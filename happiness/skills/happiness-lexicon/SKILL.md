# Happiness Lexicon

This skill helps you produce nuanced, culturally-grounded answers when the user asks for vocabulary around happiness, joy, contentment, or related states across languages.

## Where the lexicon lives

The curated lexicon is included at the end of this file. Read it at the start of any conversation where this skill triggers — it is short enough to load fully. If an older copy also exists in `references/lexicon.md`, the version in this file supersedes it.

## How to answer

Listen first for the *texture* the user is reaching for. Happiness vocabulary is dense, and the right word depends on:

- **Register** — domestic, philosophical, religious, archaic, colloquial, literary
- **Intensity** — a settled background state vs. a sudden burst vs. an absorbed transcendence
- **Sociality** — solitary, dyadic, communal
- **Temporal shape** — sustained, momentary, anticipatory, retrospective
- **Emotional admixture** — pure, bittersweet, melancholy-tinged, longing-laced

Then pick **2–5 words** that map most precisely. Don't dump the lexicon. Curation is the value.

For each word, give:

1. The word in its original script (with transliteration if non-Latin)
2. The language
3. A one-line gloss of its specific nuance — what it captures that the user's default word doesn't
4. Optionally: a register marker (literary, colloquial, archaic, philosophical), or a brief example phrase or literary echo

## Style

Keep entries tight. The point is precision, not exoticism. Avoid the "untranslatable word" trope — most "untranslatable" words are translatable; what's distinctive is their *frequency*, *register*, or *cultural prominence*. Be honest about this when relevant.

If the user asks about happiness in a single language, go deeper into that language's gradient (4–8 words, with relations between them) rather than spreading thin across many.

If the user is reaching for a feeling that isn't quite happiness — *saudade*, *hygge*, *ikigai*, *hiraeth*, *naḥat* — the boundary case is precisely the interesting part. Offer it.

## What to avoid

- Romanticizing or mystifying non-European languages
- Listing words without explaining their distinct semantic territory
- Pretending two near-synonyms occupy actually different ground
- Citing dubious folk etymologies

## Extending the lexicon

If the user asks about a language not in the lexicon below, answer from your general knowledge but flag that it's outside the curated set. Offer to add it as a new entry if they want.

# The lexicon

Curated entries by language. Adjacent terms (longing, ease, flourishing, blessedness) included where they share semantic territory with happiness.

## Italian

- **felicità** — happiness as a state, often idealized, sometimes carrying the weight of its own pursuit. *La ricerca della felicità*.
- **gioia** — joy: brighter, more sudden, less philosophical than *felicità*.
- **allegria** — cheerfulness, light and social; the mood of a good table.
- **contentezza** — contentment: the quiet, sufficient kind. Less intense than *felicità*, more sustainable.
- **letizia** — gladness, archaic and literary; carries Marian and Dantean echoes.
- **beatitudine** — beatitude, religious-philosophical bliss.

## English

- **happiness** — the umbrella term, often diluted by overuse.
- **joy** — sharper, more elevated, often momentary.
- **contentment** — settled, quiet, undramatic.
- **bliss** — absorbed, sometimes sensual, sometimes transcendent.
- **delight** — happiness with the texture of surprise.
- **gladness** — gentle, slightly archaic.
- **cheer** — social, light, externally directed.
- **merry** — happiness as time made short. Old English *myrge* meant "pleasing, agreeable, sweet" and was said of grass, trees, the world, music and song; of persons, "cheerful by disposition", only by the mid-14th century (Etymonline). From Proto-Germanic *\*murgijaz*, probably "short-lasting" (Old High German *murg*, "short"; Gothic *gamaurgjan*, "to shorten"), from PIE *\*mréǵʰus*, "short", the root of Latin *brevis* and Greek βραχύς (*brakhýs*, "short"): *merry* and *brief* are cousins, and *mirth* is the noun. Etymonline gives Middle Dutch *mergelijc*, "joyful", as the only exact cognate in meaning outside English. The step from "short" to "pleasant" predates the texts, so the bridge is reconstructed (the pleasant is what makes the time pass quickly); Wiktionary routes it through "slow, leisurely" instead. The short-time reading is supported by the same shift from two other words for "short": German *Kurzweil* and Old Norse *skemta*, Danish *skæmt* (see both). The earliest Germanic text with the root is Wulfila's Gothic Bible, Mark 13:20 (Codex Argenteus): ἐκολόβωσεν κύριος τὰς ἡμέρας, "the Lord cut short the days" of the tribulation, becomes *gamaurgida þans dagans*, "he shortened the days", for the sake of the chosen. Old English *myrgð*, *mirth*, could also mean eternal bliss, salvation. Literary echo: Burns, "Halloween" (1785; Kilmarnock edition, 1786), a night of charms for foretelling one's future spouse. Stanza 2 gathers *Some merry, friendly, countra-folks* to burn nuts and pull kail stalks; in stanza 27 *Auld uncle John*, who has wanted *wedlock's joys* since Mar's year (1715), draws the empty dish three times and heaves the dishes on the fire; the last stanza opens *Wi' merry sangs, an' friendly cracks, / I wat they did na weary*. Stanzas 2 and 28 both end *Fu' blythe that night*. A reading, not Burns's: the charms ask about a long future, *merry* names a short present, and "they did na weary" is the etymology acted out.
- **flourishing** — modern philosophical translation of *eudaimonia*; sustained wellbeing rather than mood.

## Danish

- **lykke** — happiness in its everyday form; often the contained, domestic kind.
- **glæde** — gladness, joy; lighter and more momentary than *lykke*.
- **hygge** — not happiness as such, but the conditions for it: warmth, soft light, company, sufficiency. Cultural keyword.
- **lyksalighed** — bliss; literary, elevated.
- **skæmt** — jest, fun; the verb is *skæmte*. Not happiness but amusement, here for its etymology: Old Norse *skemta*, "to amuse, entertain", is formed on *skammr*, "short", and the Norwegian Bokmål dictionary glosses it *gjøre (tiden) kort*, "make the time short". Cognate with Swedish *skämt* and Icelandic *skemmtun*. The same shift as English *merry* and German *Kurzweil* (see both). Its partner in the fixed pair *spøg og skæmt* has a ghost in it: *spøg* comes from Middle Low German *spōk*, cognate with Dutch *spook*, "ghost" (whence English *spook*), and *spøge* still means both "to joke" and "to haunt" (*spøgelse*, ghost).

## Icelandic / Old Norse

- **hamingja** — the everyday modern Icelandic word for happiness (*hamingjusamur*, "happy"). In Old Norse it named a being rather than a feeling: the personified luck of a person or a family, a female guardian spirit that passed at death to a beloved kinsman and could be lent — *leggja hamingju sína með e-m*, "to lay one's hamingja alongside someone", which Cleasby–Vigfússon liken to giving one's blessing. Cleasby–Vigfússon derive the word from *hamr*, "skin, shape" (the same element as Danish *ham* and *legeme*, German *Leichnam*). The often-cited reconstruction *\*ham-gengja*, "she who walks in (another's) shape", has not been verified against an etymological dictionary: present it as a hypothesis. Locus classicus: *Víga-Glúms saga* ch. 9, where Glúmr dreams of a woman taller than the mountains walking up the valley, invites her in, and reads her as his dead grandfather's hamingja seeking a *staðfesta* (a fixed dwelling) where he is.
- Old Norse near-synonyms for luck and fortune: *gæfa*, *gipta*, *auðna*, *heill*.

## French

- **bonheur** — happiness as a sustained condition; weighty, philosophical.
- **joie** — joy, vivid and bright.
- **allégresse** — cheerful, often communal joy; the mood of a feast.
- **béatitude** — beatitude, transcendent.
- **félicité** — felicity, slightly archaic, formal.
- **contentement** — contentment, modest.

## German

- **Glück** — happiness *and* luck. The same word for both is itself instructive.
- **Freude** — joy.
- **Vorfreude** — anticipatory joy; the pleasure that consists entirely in looking forward, often held to exceed the joy of the event itself.
- **Kurzweil** — pastime, amusement; literally "a short while", against *Langeweile*, boredom, "a long while". The noun is dated (Duden: *veraltend*); the adjectives are everyday, *kurzweilig*, "entertaining", against *langweilig*, "boring". Not happiness but amusement, measured by how short the time felt: the same shift English made, out of sight, in *merry* (see English).
- **Seligkeit** — beatitude; carries religious overtones.
- **Wonne** — delight, almost sensual.
- **Heiterkeit** — serenity, untroubled cheer; the quality of a clear sky.
- **Zufriedenheit** — contentment, satisfaction.

## Spanish

- **felicidad** — happiness.
- **alegría** — joy, cheerfulness; central, frequent.
- **dicha** — happiness that was luck first, and luck that was speech first. From Latin *dicta*, "things said", which in popular usage took on the sense of *fatum* (itself from *fari*, "to speak"); the DLE traces this to the pagan belief that each person's lot was the words the gods pronounced at birth. The older senses survive in *a/por dicha*, "by chance", and in hunting usage, where a *dicha* is a hound's bark on the trail. Warm, slightly literary; alive in *¡Dichosos los ojos!*, said to someone not seen in too long. Its pair is *ventura* (Latin *ventura*, "what is to come"; cf. *aventura*): fortune pronounced against fortune on its way. Literary echo: *Don Quijote* I, 11, where the Golden Age speech opens *Dichosa edad y siglos dichosos…* and calls that age *venturosa* in the same sentence. The knight delivers it over acorns to goatherds who have just shared their supper with two strangers; Cervantes says it could well have been spared. At the chapter's end a goatherd dresses the ear a Basque's sword had half carried off (I, 9) with chewed rosemary and salt, and says he will need no other medicine: *y así fue la verdad*. A reading, not Cervantes's: the chapter's one *dicha* in the old sense, words spoken over someone that then come true, is the goatherd's.
- **júbilo** — jubilation.
- **gozo** — enjoyment, often spiritual or aesthetic.

## Portuguese

- **felicidade** — happiness.
- **alegria** — joy.
- **saudade** — the bittersweet longing for what is absent; happiness through loss, or in spite of loss. Adjacent to but distinct from happiness — and one of the most semantically distinctive entries in this lexicon.

## Ancient Greek

- **εὐδαιμονία** *(eudaimonia)* — flourishing; Aristotle's term for a life going well as a whole, not a feeling.
- **μακαρία** *(makaria)* — blessedness; the happiness of the gods, of the dead, of the truly fortunate.
- **χαρά** *(khara)* — joy.
- **εὐφροσύνη** *(euphrosyne)* — mirth, festive cheer.
- **θαλία** *(thalia)* — abundance, festive good cheer; from *thallein*, "to bloom, flourish". One of the three Graces bears the name; plenty understood as flowering rather than accumulation.
- **ἡδονή** *(hēdonē)* — pleasure; for the Epicureans, the highest good (correctly understood as absence of pain).

## Latin

- **felicitas** — happiness, fortune; Roman virtue and goddess.
- **beatitudo** — beatitude; Augustinian and Boethian key term.
- **gaudium** — joy, often interior or spiritual.
- **laetitia** — gladness, often outwardly visible.

## Arabic

- **سعادة** *(saʿāda)* — happiness; the standard term.
- **فرح** *(faraḥ)* — joy, celebration.
- **سرور** *(surūr)* — delight, pleasure.
- **بهجة** *(bahja)* — splendor, radiant joy.
- **طمأنينة** *(ṭumaʾnīna)* — settled tranquility; the heart at rest. Quranic resonance.
- **طرب** *(ṭarab)* — the enchantment that music brings on. A boundary case, and the counterpart of *ṭumaʾnīna*: the heart moved rather than the heart at rest. The classical dictionaries give it no direction. Al-Jawharī's *Ṣiḥāḥ* defines it as *khiffa tuṣību l-insāna li-shiddati ḥuznin aw surūr*, "a lightness that befalls a person from the force of grief or joy". The *Lisān al-ʿArab* adds Thaʿlab's "joy and grief", the sense *shawq* (longing), and Thaʿlab's "for me ṭarab is movement", which Ibn Sīda says he does not know. Al-Zamakhsharī's *Asās al-Balāgha*: camels feel ṭarab at the caravan song, and *al-karīm ṭarūb*, "the generous are easily moved". The singer is the *muṭrib*, the one who brings ṭarab. Locus (legend, not history): in Ibn Khallikān's *Wafayāt al-aʿyān*, al-Fārābī plays a few pieces of wood in three assemblies at Sayf al-Dawla's court, and the room laughs, weeps, then sleeps, doorkeeper included, while he walks out; al-Dhahabī's *Siyar* drops the weeping and has them "laugh from ṭarab". The same al-Fārābī wrote *Taḥṣīl al-saʿāda*, *The Attainment of Happiness*: *saʿāda* is attained, ṭarab befalls (a reading, not his).

## Classical Chinese

- **樂** *(yuè / lè / yào)* — music, joy, and delighting-in, all written with one graph that draws an instrument (strings on a wooden frame in Luo Zhenyu's reading of the oracle bones; drums on a stand in the *Shuowen*). Xunzi: 樂者，樂也, "music is joy." Joy as something played and practised rather than something that befalls you; the counterweight to *lykke* and *Glück*. In the Analects' opening passage it is the joy of friends arriving from afar, paired with 說 *yuè*, the delight of study; Zhu Xi reads the pair by whether the feeling shows: delight kept in the heart, joy that goes outward. Literary echo: Confucius in Qi, three months not knowing the taste of meat after hearing the Shao (Analects 7.14), three months of study in the *Shiji*'s version. The 楽 of Japanese *tanoshimi*.

## Japanese

- **幸せ** *(shiawase)* — happiness, often quiet and personal.
- **幸** *(sachi)* — happiness that began as hunting and fishing gear, and the power in it. Today mostly in set phrases (*umi no sachi*, *yama no sachi*, "the yield of the sea, of the mountains"; *sachi are*, "may there be happiness") and as a given name; the *Kojiki* (712) spells it phonetically, 佐知. The *Nihon kokugo daijiten* lists its senses in this order: the gear for taking game or fish, and the spirit-power it holds; an abundant catch, or the catch itself; happiness. Its usage note calls the first two original: *sachi* was unrelated to *saki* (幸), happiness as a state, and took on that sense because getting *sachi* led to *saki* and the two sounded almost alike (only the place of articulation of the second syllable's voiceless consonant differs). Old Japanese texts have no certain example of *sachi* as happiness outside hunting and fishing. The combining form *satsu-* keeps to the hunt: *satsuyumi*, hunting bow; *satsuhito*, hunter. Chamberlain, the *Kojiki*'s first English translator (1882), read the semantics the other way round: "luck, happiness" first, then a man's forte, finally what he procures and the implements he procures it with. Not the same word as 幸せ *(shiawase)*, older 仕合わせ, which only borrowed the character. Locus classicus: *Kojiki*, end of Book 1. Hoderi is *umi-sachi-biko*, the man with the sea's *sachi*, his younger brother Hoori *yama-sachi-biko*. Takeda Yūkichi's commentary glosses *sachi* as a power dwelling in the tool, so that whoever holds it takes much game, and his modern translation turns the brothers' exchange of *sachi* into an exchange of 道具, gear. Hoori fishes with the sea-*sachi*, catches nothing and loses the hook, and Hoderi wants it back: 山幸もおのが幸幸。海幸もおのが幸幸。 *(yamasachi mo ono ga sachisachi, umisachi mo ono ga sachisachi)*, "the mountain's *sachi* is its owner's *sachi*, the sea's *sachi* is its owner's *sachi*" (Chamberlain: "a luck of its own"; he warns that the next clause, "let each of us now restore his luck", is a little confused in the original). He refuses five hundred hooks forged from Hoori's sword, then a thousand: he wants the original. In the *Nihon shoki* (720) neither brother gets his *sachi*, written there 利, "gain". Hoori finds the hook in the sea god's palace, stuck in a *tai*'s throat, and returns it as the god instructs: calling it a dull, wild, poor, grieving hook (*obochi, susuchi, majichi, uruchi*; glosses after Takeda) and handing it over behind his back. Takeda: speaking ill of the hook makes its *sachi* leave. Hoderi grows ever poorer; Hoori's son will father Jinmu, first emperor of tradition. A reading, not the *Kojiki*'s: the story thinks like the modern dictionary, not like Chamberlain. Its happiness is lodged in an instrument and answers one hand, which is why a thousand new hooks are not worth the old one; *hamingja* could be lent (see Icelandic / Old Norse), *sachi* catches nothing in borrowed hands.
- **喜び** *(yorokobi)* — joy.
- **楽しみ** *(tanoshimi)* — enjoyment, especially the pleasurable anticipation of something to come.
- **満足** *(manzoku)* — satisfaction, sufficiency.
- **生きがい** *(ikigai)* — reason for being. Not happiness itself, but its source.

## Sanskrit / Pali

- **सुख** *(sukha)* — happiness, ease. The original sense is mechanical: a wheel-axle that turns smoothly. Its opposite, *duḥkha*, is a wheel that grinds.
- **आनन्द** *(ānanda)* — bliss; in Vedanta, an attribute of ultimate reality.
- **मुदिता** *(muditā)* — sympathetic joy; one of the four Buddhist *brahmavihārās* — happiness *at others' happiness*.
- **प्रीति** *(prīti)* — rapture, the meditative joy that arises before deeper calm.

## Hebrew

- **אושר** *(osher)* — happiness.
- **שמחה** *(simcha)* — joy, especially shared and celebratory; "to make a simcha" is to throw a celebration.
- **נחת** *(naḥat)* — quiet contentment, especially the parental kind: pride and ease taken from another's flourishing.

## Welsh

- **hiraeth** — homesickness for a place that may never have existed; longing colored with belonging. Boundary case to happiness.
- **hapusrwydd** — happiness.

## Swedish / Finnish

- **lycka** *(Swedish)* / **onnellisuus** *(Finnish)* — happiness.
- **glädje** *(Swedish)* / **ilo** *(Finnish)* — joy.
- **lagom** *(Swedish)* — "just the right amount"; happiness through proportion. Cultural keyword.
- **sisu** *(Finnish)* — perseverance, grit. Not happiness, but the texture that lets it survive hardship.

## Korean

- **행복** *(haengbok)* — happiness.
- **기쁨** *(gippeum)* — joy.
- **정** *(jeong)* — deep affection, bond. The soil out of which happiness grows in Korean usage.

## Cross-index: the stages of a paper or project

An index into the entries above rather than a new set of languages. Each stage of a research project has a characteristic texture of happiness, and naming it makes the lexicon usable as a working diagnostic: when a project goes dead, ask which stage's happiness has gone missing.

- **Question** — *प्रीति (prīti)*, the rapture that arises early. In the jhāna sequence it precedes *sukha*, which is structurally apt: the joy of a good question is the joy of something not yet settled. Secondary: *Vorfreude* — a question worth having is one you look forward to being inside.
- **Dataset** — *満足 (manzoku)*, satisfaction as sufficiency: *n* is large enough, the metadata is complete. Secondary: *hygge*, in the strict sense of conditions rather than feeling — a well-tended corpus is a room you are glad to sit in. For sampling decisions specifically, *lagom*.
- **Method** — *सुख (sukha)*, on the traditional derivation the axle that turns smoothly, against *duḥkha*, the axle that grinds. The opposite of a happy method is not a sad one but a grinding one. Secondary: *Heiterkeit*, the clear sky — a method with no hidden clouds. The workmanlike version is *Zufriedenheit*.
- **Hypothesis** — *Glück*, happiness and luck in one word, which is the exact condition of staking a claim before you know. Secondary: *dicha*, from Latin *dicta*, things said: fortune as something pronounced in advance.
- **Possible outcomes** — *मुदिता (muditā)*, gladness at another's good fortune. An honest outcome space contains branches where the rival account wins, and the capacity to be genuinely pleased in those branches is what keeps the space honest rather than decorative. Secondary: *saudade* used anticipatorily — a stretch, since saudade is normally retrospective, but the outcome space does hold futures that will have to be let go.
- **Interpretation** — *طمأنينة (ṭumaʾnīna)*, the heart at rest: the reading one stops arguing with oneself about. A high bar; most first interpretations do not clear it. Secondary, as a contrasting pair: *gaudium* against *laetitia*, interior joy against outwardly visible gladness. Laetitia without gaudium means a claim written but not yet understood.

For the arc rather than the stage: *εὐδαιμονία (eudaimonia)*. A research life going well as a whole is not a mood and need not be felt on any given day.
